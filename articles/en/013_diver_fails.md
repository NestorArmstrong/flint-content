# Why Diversification Fails When It Matters Most

Diversification is often described as the only *"free lunch"* when it comes to investing. Just spread your capital across different assets and all risk falls. That principle sounds logical, but it’s incomplete. 

During the collapse of Lehman Brothers in 2008, everything declined together. US stocks, international markets, financial shares and commodities moved in the same exact direction: ***downwards***. If you wanted to find a safe place to park your money on such tumultuous time, the answer was: nowhere.

In March 2020, as the pandemic spread, the S&P 500 fell more than 30% in a matter of weeks. International markets followed suit, oil collapsed, and even gold briefly declined. Correlations surged because investors were not reallocating capital. They were simply trying to take out all of their cash at once.

This is the crude reality of crises. When liquidity disappears, investments that appear uncorrelated in normal conditions begin to move together. Investors sell what they can, and not necessarily what they want. Putting your money across many investments offers limited protection if said assets are exposed to the same risk: broad market sentiment.

Owning several technology companies or multiple equity funds may feel diversified, but if it remains concentrated in growth expectations, you may be in for a world of pain when a crisis arrives. True diversification requires exposure to investments that behave differently under stress. 

For example, high quality government bonds, cash and short duration instruments have historically provided a steady counterbalance in major downturns. Long term Treasuries, such as those tracked by the iShares 20+ Year Treasury Bond ETF (TLT), have often gained during severe equity drawdowns, though not in every single environment.

## So how does an investor protect against market crashes?

Protection comes from structuring a portfolio that assumes crises can and will happen. This means holding investments that can provide liquidity when the market falls. It’s also keeping enough stability to avoid forced selling. And above all, you want to design for permanence rather than for the absolute maximum returns.

Because the lesson is not that diversification fails. It’s that many portfolios are diversified in appearance rather than in risk. Diversification does not eliminate losses, it buys *resilience*.

And it’s resilience what determines survival.
